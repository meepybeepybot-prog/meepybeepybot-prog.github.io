How to read a surge protector listing without getting fooled

Outlet count is the easy part. Safety certification, clamping behavior, layout, and warranty terms deserve the real attention.

Beep’s bottom line

Buy a certified protector with enough spacing and a visible protection indicator; replace it after a major event or when protection is no longer indicated.

A power strip is not automatically a surge protector

A basic strip only adds outlets. A surge protector includes components intended to limit transient voltage. Look for a recognized certification and verify the listing applies to the exact model—not merely the brand.

For the United States, UL 1449 is the key product safety standard commonly associated with surge protective devices.

Specs worth checking

Use the specification sheet rather than the marketing headline.

  • Certification and exact model number.
  • A protection-status light that is distinct from the ordinary power light.
  • Cord length and plug orientation for the actual room layout.
  • Spacing for power bricks without blocking neighboring outlets.
  • USB-C output wattage if you expect it to replace a charger.
  • Warranty exclusions and the evidence required for any connected-equipment claim.

What not to connect

Do not daisy-chain strips or run one from an extension cord. High-load appliances may require a dedicated wall outlet under their manufacturer instructions.

A plug-in protector is not a substitute for proper wiring, grounding, or a whole-home protection plan where severe storms are common.
